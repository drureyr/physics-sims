One of the great laws of nature

The law of conservation of energy says energy is never created or destroyed. It only changes form. Three people, working separately in the 1840s, are usually given credit for discovering it.

Julius Robert Mayer (1814–1878)

A German ship's doctor. On a voyage to the tropics in 1840, he noticed that sailors' blood in their veins was unusually bright red. He reasoned that in a hot climate the body burns less food to stay warm. That led him to the idea that heat and motion are forms of one thing. He published the idea in 1842, but at first few scientists paid attention.

James Prescott Joule (1818–1889)

An English brewer and scientist who made careful measurements in the 1840s showing that a fixed amount of work always produces the same amount of heat. The unit of energy, the joule, is named after him.

Hermann von Helmholtz (1821–1894)

A German physician and physicist. In 1847 he published On the Conservation of Force, which stated the law mathematically for mechanics, heat, electricity and magnetism, and even living things.

Emmy Noether (1882–1935)

A German mathematician who proved in 1915 (published 1918) a deep reason why energy is conserved: because the laws of physics are the same today as they were yesterday and will be tomorrow. Albert Einstein called her a creative mathematical genius.

Connections

Biology: Mayer's idea started with human blood. Your body follows the same law: the chemical energy in food becomes the kinetic energy of your muscles and the thermal energy that keeps you at 37 °C.

Engineering: Roller coaster designers use conservation of energy. The first hill must be the tallest, because the cars can never rise higher than the energy they started with allows.

Philosophy: Noether's theorem links a law of physics to a symmetry of time itself. Many physicists see this as one of the most beautiful ideas in science.
