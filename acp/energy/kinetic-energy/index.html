The great "vis viva" debate

For about 50 years, scientists argued over the right way to measure the "force" of a moving object. Was it mv (what we now call momentum) or mv²? Both turned out to be useful, but they measure different things.

Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (1646–1716)

The German philosopher and mathematician (and co-inventor of calculus) argued in 1686 that the true measure of a moving body's "living force," or vis viva, was mv², not mv as followers of René Descartes believed.

Willem 's Gravesande (1688–1742)

A Dutch scientist who, in the early 1720s, dropped brass balls into soft clay. A ball dropped fast enough to hit at twice the speed made a dent four times as deep. Evidence for v²!

Émilie du Châtelet (1706–1749)

A French mathematician and physicist who used 's Gravesande's clay experiments to defend mv² in her book Foundations of Physics (1740). She also translated Isaac Newton's Principia into French, and her translation is still used today.

Coriolis and the "½" (1829)

Gaspard-Gustave de Coriolis (1792–1843) showed that the quantity ½mv² fits exactly with his definition of work. The name "kinetic energy" (from the Greek kinesis, motion) was made popular in the 1860s by William Thomson, Lord Kelvin (1824–1907), and Peter Guthrie Tait (1831–1901).

Connections

Driver's ed: A car going twice as fast has four times the kinetic energy, so it needs about four times the distance to stop. That's why speed limits drop near schools.

Earth science: Meteors hit the atmosphere at speeds of 11 to 72 km/s. Because KE grows with v², even a pebble-sized meteor carries enough energy to glow white-hot as a "shooting star."
