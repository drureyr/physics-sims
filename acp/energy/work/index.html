Where did the idea of "work" come from?

In everyday English, "work" means any effort. In physics it has a precise meaning: a force does work only when the object moves, and only the part of the force along the motion counts.

Gaspard-Gustave de Coriolis (1792–1843)

A French engineer and mathematician. In 1829, in his book On the Calculation of the Effect of Machines, he gave the word travail ("work") its scientific meaning: force multiplied by the distance moved in the direction of the force. He wanted a fair way to measure what a machine actually accomplishes.

Jean-Victor Poncelet (1788–1867)

A French engineer and army officer who taught mechanics to engineers. Like Coriolis, he championed the idea of "mechanical work" in the 1820s and 1830s. He worked out many of his geometry ideas while a prisoner of war in Russia after Napoleon's failed invasion of 1812.

James Watt (1736–1819)

The Scottish inventor whose improved steam engine (patented 1769) helped power the Industrial Revolution. To sell engines, he compared them to horses and invented the unit horsepower. The SI unit of power, the watt (1 joule of work per second), is named after him.

Connections

History and engineering: The idea of "work" grew out of the Industrial Revolution. Factory owners needed to compare water wheels, horses and steam engines, so they had to measure how much useful work each one did.

Biology: When you hold a heavy backpack still, your muscles get tired, but you do no physics work on the backpack because it doesn't move. Your muscle fibers are still using food energy to keep contracting.
