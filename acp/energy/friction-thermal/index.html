Where does the energy go?

Real objects slow down and stop. For a long time, scientists thought heat was an invisible fluid called "caloric." Experiments with friction proved them wrong and showed that the "lost" energy becomes thermal energy.

Benjamin Thompson, Count Rumford (1753–1814)

Born in Woburn, Massachusetts, not far from Newton! He sided with the British in the American Revolution, then moved to Bavaria (Germany), where he oversaw the boring (drilling) of cannons. In 1798 he noticed that a dull drill could produce heat endlessly, enough to boil water, as long as horses kept turning it. Heat couldn't be a fluid that runs out. It had to come from the work of friction.

James Prescott Joule (1818–1889)

In his famous paddle-wheel experiment (1845, improved through 1850), falling weights turned a paddle wheel in a container of water. Friction between the paddles and water warmed the water by a tiny but measurable amount. The potential energy lost by the weights matched the thermal energy gained by the water.

Connections

Local history: The Rumford Fireplace and the "Rumford" brand of baking powder are both named after Count Rumford. He also left money to Harvard to fund a professorship that still bears his name.

Engineering: Car and bicycle brakes work by friction, which turns kinetic energy into thermal energy. After a long downhill drive, brakes can get hot enough to smoke.

Space science: A spacecraft returning to Earth uses air friction to slow down from about 7,800 m/s. Its heat shield must survive temperatures over 1,500 °C.
