Connecting work and motion

By 1800, engineers measured work (force × distance) and scientists argued about vis viva (mv²). The work–energy theorem connected the two: the work done on an object equals the change in its kinetic energy.

Gaspard-Gustave de Coriolis (1792–1843)

In 1829 Coriolis showed that if you define work as force × distance, then the work done on a body equals the change in ½mv². That is exactly why kinetic energy has the "½" in it. (Coriolis is also famous for the "Coriolis effect," which makes hurricanes spin.)

Thomas Young (1773–1829)

An English doctor and scientist (he also helped decode the Egyptian hieroglyphs on the Rosetta Stone). In 1807 he was the first to use the word "energy" in its modern scientific sense, for the quantity mv².

Connections

Sports: A baseball pitcher pushes the ball forward over about 2 meters during the throw. The work done by the hand becomes the ball's kinetic energy. A longer stride means a longer push distance, more work and a faster pitch.

Engineering: Car brakes do negative work. They push backward on the car while it moves forward, which removes kinetic energy. Highway "runaway truck ramps" use gravel and an uphill slope to do the same thing.
