Gravitational Potential Energy: Energy of Height

Gravitational potential energy
PE = m g h
Weight, mg (lifting force)
Height
Work to lift = mg × h
Potential energy, PE

The block is lifted at a slow, steady speed, so the lifting force equals its weight. Heights are measured from the ground (PE = 0 at the ground).

Energy that is "stored," waiting to be used

A raised object can do work when it comes down: it can turn a water wheel, drive a pile into the ground or run a clock. Scientists needed a name for this hidden, stored energy.

William Rankine (1820–1872)

A Scottish engineer who studied steam engines and the strength of materials. In 1853 he introduced the term "potential energy" for energy stored by position, as opposed to "actual" (kinetic) energy of motion.

Christiaan Huygens (1629–1695)

The Dutch scientist who built the first accurate pendulum clock in 1656. Weight-driven clocks run on potential energy: a raised weight slowly falls and keeps the clock ticking for days. Huygens also noticed that a swinging pendulum can rise back to the height it started from, but no higher.

Connections

Engineering and history: The Hoover Dam (completed 1936) holds back Lake Mead. Water falling about 150 meters through the dam's turbines turns potential energy into electricity for millions of people. "Pumped storage" power plants pump water uphill at night and let it fall during the day.

Earth science: Rocks on a steep mountainside store huge amounts of potential energy. In a landslide or rockfall, that energy is released all at once.

Space science: Try the Moon and Jupiter! The same block at the same height stores very different energy, because the gravitational field strength g is different.
